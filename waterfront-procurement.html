Fixing the Crocker Park dock

A July rainstorm broke a flotation log free beneath the Crocker Park dock, and the town closed one of its most popular swimming spots. The obvious next step is to call the contractor who has always fixed the town's docks and have him fix it. The town cannot just do that anymore. Why not is a small window into a bigger change running through the town – the handshake giving way to the process – that is genuinely better and worse at the same time.

The situation

The town's docks and floats are put in, pulled out, and repaired by one contractor: Marblehead Marine Construction, the trade name of David Haley Inc.

"Dave Haley moves all our docks."Harbors and Waters Board meeting record, February 2023

He is also the one who chases down docks that break loose – when the Village Street dock washed to Kettle Cove in Magnolia in 2023, he brought it back – and he has donated a float to the town. In fiscal year 2026 the town paid his business $47,846.75 across 13 payments, all coded "Float work Blanket PO" – a standing account it draws on as needed. No competitive bid stands behind it in any record we found; the payments are in the FY26 checkbook. So when the dock breaks, the obvious move is to call him. Whether the town is allowed to is the catch.

Why it is not that simple

Paying a contractor for a service is procurement, and state law – Chapter 30B, the Uniform Procurement Act – sets the process by the price:

Value of the workWhat the law requires
Under $10,000Informal, no set process.
$10,000–$50,000Written quotes from at least three vendors.
Over $50,000A formal, advertised bid.

The price that counts is the whole job, not a single visit. A town cannot split a large job into a stream of small payments to stay under a limit; that is illegal "bid-splitting." So about $48,000 of float work a year counts as one $48,000 job – in the three-quotes range, near the line where a full bid is required.

Frequent small payments are not automatically wrong; they can make sense for work that is seasonal and hard to predict, as dock work is. But the town has to be able to justify the arrangement, and there is no exemption for simply trusting a contractor. Weighing that is the job of the town's first full-time procurement officer, hired in 2025. It is why the town cannot just cut a check for the repair without asking the question first.

What the town did

The town's Chief Procurement Officer, Allison Jenkins, laid it out in a July 2026 reply to a resident: the urgent safety work and the permanent repair were handled as two separate jobs.

Right away, the town secured the site, removing the gangway and making the dock inaccessible. That cost under $10,000, so it needed no bidding.

The permanent repair will cost more, so it has to be competitively bid – write a scope, post it, take quotes, award the work. Jenkins said the delay was never about whether the dock would be fixed, only about following the rules for the permanent job. By late July the dock had been pulled from the water and the town had posted the job, with bids due August 13, 2026; the harbormaster said repairs were still weeks away.

One change would stop the next storm from restarting the clock: bid a multi-year dock-maintenance contract once, so future repairs draw on an agreement already in place.

What is still unknown

  1. Whether the standing arrangement has ever been bid. The current repair is being quoted competitively, but the ~$48,000-a-year blanket order is a separate question, and nothing in the record we reviewed shows it was ever competitively procured or has a documented business reason.
  2. How much the town has paid over time. FY2026 was $47,846.75; the town's open-finance portal did not return earlier years.
  3. Who gets the job. With bids due August 13, no contract was awarded. The longtime float contractor could win it.

The end of the handshake

The dock is one small sign of a change running through the whole town: the handshake giving way to the process. Much of Marblehead's public waterfront was built by private hands – Crocker Park itself was donated to the town in 1885 – and for generations its upkeep ran on informal arrangements: volunteers, donations, and a trusted contractor on a handshake. That is being replaced, on purpose, by formal bidding and managed liability.

Residents still pitch in, but the offers now run through paperwork. A resident privately repaired the failing Fort Beach steps in 2022, routed through a permit and a donation account. When both waterfront boards said they no longer wanted to maintain the State Street Landing restrooms – shut in winter for over twenty years – a residents' group offered to run them with volunteers. The board said yes, but only "providing there is no expense to the enterprise fund," and the lasting fix turned out to be a bid-out heating contract, not the volunteers.

The reason is increasingly said out loud: volunteers "cannot be used to staff the [Devereux Beach] lot because of labor requirements," and under the state's Tort Claims Act even unpaid help can expose the town, so it now arrives wrapped in permits and insurance forms. The town built the machinery on purpose, too, hiring its first full-time procurement officer in 2025 after the old patch-it-together model proved "not sustainable" and drew an audit finding. Now even a set of park stairs draws seven or eight bids.

"I work on handshakes and stuff like that, so this is difficult, but it's in the best interest of the town, and so I have to work forward on this."A department head, on putting a long-standing trash contract out to bid, April 2026

Is that better or worse for the town? Both.

Better

The same rules apply to everyone, the price is competitive and documented, and the town – not a volunteer or a handshake vendor – carries the risk when something goes wrong.

Worse

It is slower and often costlier, it sheds local knowledge and goodwill, it turns away people who just want to help – and this summer it means empty water where the dock used to be, over a repair the old way might have done in two weeks.

None of this is an accident, and little of it is likely to reverse. The town is trading the handshake for the process, one arrangement at a time, and the trade runs both ways at once: more fairness, less improvisation; more protection, less speed. The Crocker Park dock will come back, competitively bid and properly insured. It just will not come back this summer – and a summer is not a thing a town gets back.
