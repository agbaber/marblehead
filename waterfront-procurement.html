How the town hires its waterfront vendors

On the Tuesday of a July rainstorm, a flotation log beneath the Crocker Park dock broke free and the town closed one of its most popular swimming spots. Repairing it means paying someone. Who that someone is, and how the town is allowed to pay them, turns out to be an unsettled question.

The town relies on two private businesses on its waterfront. It competed one of them for a license worth a few thousand dollars. It pays the other roughly ten times as much, every year, with no competition at all. Both facts are in the public record. The storm arrived the same season the town hired its first full-time procurement officer, whose job is to bring arrangements like the second one into a formal process.

The dock work: paid, and not bid

The town's docks and floats are installed, removed, and repaired by Marblehead Marine Construction, the trade name of David Haley Inc. The Harbors and Waters Board's own meeting record describes the arrangement plainly: "Dave Haley moves all our docks." That includes the Crocker Park float specifically: in September 2022 the board was "waiting to hear from Dave Haley on the rest of the float removal." When a dock has broken loose before, he is the one who retrieved it: in January 2023, after the Village Street dock "broke away and was found in Kettle Cove in Magnolia," the record notes that "Dave Haley of Marblehead Marine brought the dock back." He has also given money back to the town: a 2023 Recreation and Parks report records that "Dave Haley has contributed $2,500 for a new float at Stramski's."

In fiscal year 2026, the town paid Marblehead Marine Construction $47,846.75 across 13 payments, drawn from the Harbor Enterprise fund. Nearly every line reads "Float work Blanket PO." A blanket purchase order is a standing account a department draws against for recurring work without writing a separate order each time. It is a routine tool. What makes this one notable is the total and the type of work: roughly $48,000 of construction and repair service, to one vendor, with no bid behind it. (This is a different business from Marblehead Marine Inc., the Front Street fuel dock, which the town paid about $1,900 for boat fuel the same year; the similar names are easy to confuse. See the town's FY26 checkbook to trace the payments.)

The paddleboard license: competed

The other waterfront business is SUP East Coast Style, the stand-up paddleboard and kayak rental operation that has run a kiosk at Riverhead Beach since 2009. Unlike the dock contractor, this business pays the town rather than being paid by it, for the right to operate on public land. And unlike the dock contractor, the town put that right out to competition.

In July 2024 the Recreation and Parks Commission decided to solicit proposals for the concession, with the then-procurement officer set to "send out the RFP to potential bidders." A request for proposals is a public, sealed-bid solicitation. The notice went out in December 2025, and in January 2026 the commission awarded the license to SUP East Coast Style at $6,180 a year. Two proposals came in; only the paddleboard operator fit the Riverhead space. By the following meeting the commission noted the beach "leases will be released soon to allow the rental businesses to function during the warm season."

What the procurement law requires

Paying a vendor for a service is governed by Chapter 30B, the state's Uniform Procurement Act, which sets the process by the dollar amount:

Contract sizeWhat the law requires
Under $10,000"Sound business practices" – informal, no set process.
$10,000–$50,000Written quotes from at least three vendors.
Over $50,000A formal, advertised sealed bid or request for proposals.

At roughly $48,000 a year, the dock work sits in the middle band, where written quotes from three vendors are the standard, and just under the line where a formal sealed bid would be. Massachusetts does not offer a general "sole source" exemption for services the way federal rules do: being the only contractor a town wants to use does not, by itself, remove the requirement to seek quotes. There is, however, an emergency provision. Chapter 30B allows a procurement officer to skip the normal process "whenever the time required to comply with a requirement of this chapter would endanger the health or safety of the people or their property," provided the work is limited to the emergency and documented afterward. A closed, damaged dock that people can still reach from the water is the kind of situation that provision was written for.

The town created its first dedicated, full-time chief procurement officer in 2025, a role the town administrator described as covering "capital projects" and "enforcement of construction contracts," not just office supplies. Formalizing long-informal vendor relationships is precisely what that office exists to do, and the town has been doing it elsewhere: the Harbors and Waters Board itself works within the thresholds on other jobs, keeping a yacht-club furnace replacement "below $10,000" after "soliciting multiple estimates," and has used emergency fund transfers before the Finance Committee for urgent waterfront repairs.

Three ways to read it

Residents disagree about what the two arrangements add up to. Three readings, each consistent with the same facts.

The efficiency reading

The town has one experienced local contractor who knows its specific docks, retrieves them when they break loose, responds fast, and has even donated toward a town float. Forcing a formal bid on work only one nearby business realistically does adds paperwork, and possibly cost and delay, without producing real competition. The handshake has kept the docks working.

The consistency reading

The town made a small paddleboard operator compete in public for a $6,180 license, but a $48,000-a-year marine-service arrangement has never been bid. The same principle points in opposite directions on the same waterfront. If competition is the rule, it should apply to the larger, taxpayer-funded contract at least as much as to the smaller one that pays the town.

The compliance reading

Chapter 30B is not optional, and it keys off the dollar amount, not the town's comfort with a vendor. Roughly $48,000 in service work is above the informal threshold. A new procurement officer reviewing that line and asking for quotes is doing the assigned job. If the broken dock is a genuine hazard, the emergency provision is the lawful way to act now and formalize the rest later.

Open questions

  1. Was the dock arrangement flagged, and is Recreation and Parks blocked from paying for the repair? Residents have described the new procurement officer flagging the float arrangement and the department being told it cannot pay for the Crocker repair without a procurement process. As of publication, that account is not documented in any public meeting record or town document this project could locate. It remains unverified. The Harbors and Waters Board's published observer record ends in January 2024, and the recent Recreation and Parks records do not mention it.
  2. Is dock repair a "service" or "construction"? If the work is public construction rather than a marine service, a different statute applies, with its own thresholds and state prevailing-wage rules that attach at any dollar amount. Which category dock installation and repair falls into changes both the process and the cost, and is the kind of judgment the procurement officer exists to make.
  3. How much has the town paid over time? The FY26 figure is $47,846.75. The town's open-finance portal did not return earlier years to this project's queries, so whether this has been a steady ~$48,000-a-year arrangement or varies is not yet established from the primary record.
  4. What happens to the Crocker Park dock now? The harbormaster said in July he was still assessing the damage. Whether the repair goes out for quotes, runs through the emergency provision, or waits is unresolved.
