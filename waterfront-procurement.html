Fixing the Crocker Park dock

A July rainstorm broke a flotation log free beneath the Crocker Park dock, and the town closed one of its most popular swimming spots. The obvious next step is to call the contractor who has always fixed the town's docks and have him fix it. The town cannot quite do that, and the reason is worth understanding.

The situation

The town's docks and floats are installed, removed, and repaired by Marblehead Marine Construction, the trade name of David Haley Inc. The Harbors and Waters Board's own meeting record puts it plainly: "Dave Haley moves all our docks." That has included the Crocker Park float specifically, and when a dock has broken loose before, he is the one who brought it back: after the Village Street dock "broke away and was found in Kettle Cove in Magnolia" in January 2023, the record notes that "Dave Haley of Marblehead Marine brought the dock back." He has also given back, contributing "$2,500 for a new float at Stramski's."

In fiscal year 2026 the town paid Marblehead Marine Construction $47,846.75 across 13 payments from the Harbor Enterprise fund, nearly all coded "Float work Blanket PO." There is no competitive bid behind that arrangement; the payments are traceable in the town's FY26 checkbook. So when the dock breaks, the natural move is to have him repair it. Whether the town can simply do that is the unsettled part.

Why it is not that simple

Paying a vendor for a service is procurement, governed by Chapter 30B, the state's Uniform Procurement Act. The rules key off "the estimated dollar value of the goods or services you wish to procure," and that value is the whole job, not each visit:

Estimated value of the workWhat the law requires
Under $10,000"Sound business practices" – informal, no set process.
$10,000–$50,000Written quotes from at least three vendors.
Over $50,000A formal, advertised sealed bid or request for proposals.

What matters is the total the town expects to spend on the service, not the size of any single payment. A town cannot take a large body of work and pay it out as a stream of small orders to stay under the threshold: dividing "a purchase into two or more smaller purchases for the purpose of evading the requirements of a bidding law" is bid-splitting, which the Inspector General's manual calls "illegal under Chapter 30B." So roughly $48,000 of annual float work is measured as one $48,000 procurement, in the written-quotes range and near the line where a formal bid is required, even though each individual float job is smaller.

That does not make frequent small payments automatically improper. The same manual notes that "in some circumstances, it makes good business sense to make small, frequent purchases," and dock work – seasonal, weather-driven, hard to predict – may be one of them. But the burden is on the town to "justify the practice with a good business reason," and Massachusetts offers no general "sole source" exemption that would let it skip competition simply because Haley is the contractor it trusts. Whether the float arrangement clears that bar is exactly the judgment the town's first full-time procurement officer, hired in 2025, exists to make. That is why the town cannot simply cut a check for the Crocker repair without asking the question first.

What the town can do

Fixing a broken public dock quickly and following the procurement rules are not actually in conflict. There are a few paths, and they can be combined.

Secure it now under the emergency provision. If the harbormaster judges the damaged dock a hazard, Chapter 30B lets the town hire a contractor immediately to remove or stabilize it, with no bid and a written record filed afterward. This is the fastest lawful option, and it covers only the urgent work: getting the danger out of the water. The town has used emergency fund transfers for urgent waterfront repairs before.

Get three quotes for the repair. At roughly $48,000, the repair itself falls in the band where the town needs written quotes from three vendors, not a full advertised bid. That is a matter of days or a couple of weeks, not months. The town's longtime dock contractor could still be the one who wins it; the difference is that the price now sits on paper next to two others.

Bid it formally if the rebuild is large. If a permanent replacement runs past $50,000, a formal, advertised sealed bid or request for proposals is required. That is the slowest path, and it applies to a full rebuild, not to clearing an immediate hazard.

Settle whether it is a "service" or "construction" first. Everything above assumes dock repair is a service. If the state treats it as public construction instead, a different statute sets the thresholds and state prevailing-wage rules attach at any dollar amount, changing both the process and the cost. This classification decides which of the options above applies.

Put dock work on a standing contract going forward. The reason a routine repair becomes an open question is that the town's float work has ridden a blanket order with no competitive award underneath it. The town could bid a multi-year dock-maintenance contract once, so future jobs, including the next storm, draw against an agreement that is already compliant. That does not speed up this repair, but it keeps the next one from landing in the same spot.

Open questions

  1. Is the repair actually stalled over procurement? Residents have described the new procurement officer flagging the dock arrangement and Recreation and Parks being told it cannot pay for the repair without a process. As of publication, that account is not documented in any public meeting record or town document this project could locate. It remains unverified. The Harbors and Waters Board's published observer record ends in January 2024, and recent Recreation and Parks records do not mention it.
  2. Has the town justified the blanket-order arrangement? Small, frequent purchases from one vendor are allowed with a documented "good business reason." Whether the town has one on file for the float work, and whether the ~$48,000 was ever competitively procured, is not established in the public record this project reviewed.
  3. How much has the town paid over time? The FY26 figure is $47,846.75. The town's open-finance portal did not return earlier years to this project's queries, so whether this has been a steady arrangement or varies is not yet established from the primary record.
  4. What happens to the dock now? The harbormaster said in July he was still assessing the damage. Whether the repair runs through the emergency provision, goes out for quotes, or waits is unresolved.
