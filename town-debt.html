How has Marblehead managed its debt?

Marblehead carries $116.5 million in long-term debt as of June 30, 2024. Every dollar of it was approved by voters, project by project, in 51 ballot questions since 1988. Voters have said yes to 50 of them.

Where the debt came from

Almost every capital project Marblehead has built since the late 1980s, from new schools to fire trucks to a library renovation, has been financed by a debt exclusion: a temporary property-tax surcharge voters approve at the ballot, outside the Proposition 2½ levy cap. The surcharge runs only long enough to pay the bonds for that specific project, then falls off the tax bill. The state Department of Revenue's (DOR) debt-exclusion file shows 51 such ballot questions in Marblehead since 1988.

Of the 51 ballot questions, voters approved 50 and rejected one. The lone loss was a 2002 vote on bonds for remodeling, reconstruction, and repairs at Tucker's Wharf (a town-owned wharf at the foot of State Street). The town has not put a Tucker's Wharf question back on the ballot since. Schools, drainage, seawalls, fire equipment, and the Abbot Library renovation all passed, often on the first try.

How an exclusion works, start to finish

A debt exclusion is a four-step mechanism written into Proposition 2½, the 1980 state law that caps how fast a town's property-tax levy can grow:

  1. The town identifies a project that needs to be borrowed for (a school, a fire truck, a seawall). The project goes on the ballot as a specific question: bonds to do X, in an amount not to exceed Y dollars.
  2. Voters approve the borrowing at the ballot. An exclusion needs a simple majority of voters at a town-wide election, not just a Town Meeting vote. (Town Meeting votes are separate and authorize the borrowing under state law; the ballot vote is what exempts the debt service from the Prop 2.5 levy cap.)
  3. The town issues bonds and starts the work. The annual debt service on those bonds (principal plus interest, paid each year on a fixed schedule) is added to the tax levy on top of the Prop 2.5 cap, as a separate surcharge.
  4. When the bonds finish maturing, the surcharge falls off the tax bill. Older bonds roll off as newer ones come on, which is why the total debt-service line goes up and down over time rather than only up.

The practical effect: Marblehead's annual debt service is paid almost entirely outside the operating-budget levy. The FY23 budgetary-basis financial statements (in the town's ACFR) show $11,001,616 in "Total Excluded Debt Services" against essentially the same total debt-service line, meaning roughly all of the town's debt service is on the excluded side.

A debt exclusion is a one-time, project-specific tax surcharge that voters approve and that ends when the bonds for that project are paid off. The operating override now on the June 2026 ballot is structurally different: it is a permanent increase in the levy limit. See what is the override for the distinction.

What we pay now, and what is ahead

Marblehead's total annual debt service has roughly tripled since 2002, from $3.5 million to $11.0 million, with the steepest part of the climb coming after FY2020 when the new Gerry School bonds started being paid.

Total long-term debt outstanding at end of each fiscal year. Source: FY24 ACFR, "Ratios of Outstanding Debt by Type" (page 121). Total combines governmental general obligation bonds (presented net of premiums and discounts), governmental direct borrowings, and business-type direct borrowings for the Water and Municipal Light enterprises.

The $116.5 million total at June 30, 2024 breaks down as follows:

  • General obligation bonds, governmental activities: $101.07 million principal plus $7.73 million in unamortized bond premium, for a net carrying value of $108.80 million. This is the bulk of what voters approved through debt-exclusion ballots.
  • Direct borrowings, governmental activities: $0.55 million. New in FY24, a 1.38–7.73% capital financing.
  • Water enterprise, MWRA notes: $3.46 million. Two zero-interest notes from the Massachusetts Water Resources Authority (issued 2020 and 2021, payable in ten annual installments).
  • Municipal Light, MMWEC notes: $3.70 million. A 3.24% pooled loan from the Massachusetts Municipal Wholesale Electric Company that matures in February 2034.

Bond anticipation notes ("BANs", short-term notes the town issues while it lines up permanent bond financing) added another $2.3 million outstanding at year-end.

The schedule shows three useful patterns. The principal due in FY25 ($7.54 million) and FY26 ($5.96 million) is already lower than recent total debt service because some older bonds are rolling off as new ones come on. The five-year block from FY2030 through FY2034 is the heaviest stretch on the schedule, at roughly $8.3 million per year on average, because the Gerry School bonds and the FY24 capital-bundle bonds are both still in their principal-payoff phase then. By FY2035 the load eases as the older school bonds finish maturing, and by FY2044 the current schedule is fully paid off (excluding any new borrowing voters approve between now and then).

Authorized but not yet issued

At the end of FY24 the town had a further $12.23 million of borrowing that voters had already authorized but the town had not yet sold bonds for:

  • Roads and sidewalks: $8.98 million
  • Gerry School construction: $1.62 million
  • Water projects: $1.02 million
  • Mary Alley building roof replacement: $480,000
  • Franklin Street Fire Station roof: $131,000

This is debt voters have already said yes to. When the bonds are issued the resulting debt service is added to the schedule above.

Two pieces of context that go with the chart. First, debt service as a share of spending is the standard yardstick the state Department of Local Services and bond rating agencies use, but it does not say whether the borrowing was for good reasons. A town with no recent school construction would naturally show a lower share. Second, Marblehead has held an "AAA" rating with a stable outlook from Standard & Poor's, the highest available rating for general obligation debt, throughout this period. The legal debt limit set by state law (5% of equalized valuation) was approximately $475.2 million at end of FY24, and the town was using only 12.5% of that limit.

What this means for the operating override

The override question on the June 2026 ballot is about the operating budget. It is structurally separate from debt service for two reasons:

  1. Almost all annual debt service is paid by voter-approved exclusions, outside the Prop 2.5 cap. The FY23 budgetary-basis statements showed $11.00 million of debt service paid on the excluded side against $11.00 million of total debt service. Changing the operating override does not change debt service, and reducing operating spending does not free up debt-service dollars to spend elsewhere (because they were never on the operating side to begin with).
  2. Debt service is on a contractual schedule. The maturity schedule above is the legal payoff calendar for bonds the town has already issued. The town cannot reduce it without refinancing (which it has done before, in the schedule's "Refunding bonds" line) or defaulting (which the AAA rating implies the town has no intention of doing).

The slow-moving piece worth watching is the FY2030–FY2034 stretch on the maturity schedule, which is the heaviest five-year block on the books at roughly $8.3 million in annual principal alone. Whether voters approve more borrowing between now and then (and on what scale) will shape what that line looks like by the early 2030s. The 50 / 51 record suggests voters have been willing to say yes to specific projects, but the next question (and the votes after it) will each be its own ballot.

The FY24 capital bundle (roof replacements, road and sidewalk improvements, smart panels, HVAC, salt shed, and high-school boiler) is filed under "General Operating" in the DOR file and is shown under General Government in the table.
