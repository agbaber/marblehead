How has Marblehead managed its debt?

The town owes about $116 million. Voters approved every dollar of it at the ballot, project by project. The town has had 51 of those ballot questions since 1988, and voters have said yes to 50 of them.

Voters approve borrowing one project at a time. The added tax to pay off that project runs only as long as the bonds do, then comes off the tax bill. That is a separate decision from the operating override now on the June 2026 ballot.

Where the debt came from

Almost every big thing the town has built in the last forty years, from new schools to fire trucks to a library renovation, was paid for by borrowing. To borrow, the town has to put the project on the ballot and let voters decide. That ballot question is called a debt exclusion: voters agree to add the cost of paying back the bonds onto everyone's property tax bill, outside the normal Proposition 2½ cap on how fast taxes can grow.

The state Department of Revenue (DOR) keeps a file of every debt-exclusion vote in the state. For Marblehead it lists 51 ballot questions from 1988 through 2025. Most of them were for school buildings:

Voters approved 50 of the 51. The one rejection was a 2002 question on bonds for repairs to Tucker's Wharf at the foot of State Street. The town has not put a Tucker's Wharf question back on the ballot since. Everything else, including each new school, every fire truck, the seawalls, the storm drains, and the library renovation, passed. Often on the first try.

How a borrowing vote works, start to finish

A debt exclusion is written into Proposition 2½, the 1980 state law that caps property tax growth. It works in four steps:

  1. The town puts a specific project on the ballot. Bonds for a named project, up to a stated dollar amount. (Town Meeting also has to vote on it. The ballot question is the part that lets the cost go on top of the Prop 2.5 tax cap.)
  2. Voters say yes or no. Simple majority at a town-wide election.
  3. If yes, the town issues bonds and does the work. The annual repayment on those bonds gets added to property tax bills as a separate, temporary line on top of the normal levy.
  4. When the bonds are fully paid off, the surcharge comes off the tax bill. Older bonds keep rolling off as new ones get added, which is why the total stays in roughly the same range year after year instead of just climbing.

The practical effect: in fiscal year 2023, the town's audited books showed $11.00 million of debt service, and $11.00 million of it was on the "excluded" side. In other words, every dollar of the annual debt payment came from the voter-approved exclusion surcharge, not from the regular operating-budget levy.

A debt exclusion is a one-time, project-specific tax surcharge that voters approve and that ends when the bonds for that project are paid off. The operating override on the June 2026 ballot is different: a permanent increase in the tax cap, not tied to any single project.

At the Finance Committee meeting on April 7, 2025, the Finance Director explained why debt service goes down and back up like this:

"If you look at our debt service line in the budget on ClearGov, for example, you'll see a significant drop in our annual debt service from last year's budget to this year. What that means is something rolled off, right? So when you issue debt, maybe you issue it for a 15-year or 20-year. Eventually that debt service, the annual payment, will roll off. When that happens, the roll off would result in a reduced tax bill. The balancing act is when things roll off, do we need other things to roll on because of need?"

Marblehead Finance Committee meeting, April 7, 2025. Vimeo recording (1:13:45).

The same meeting noted a town policy worth knowing about: the Select Board's adopted goal is to keep total debt payments below 15% of general fund revenue. Marblehead has been around 9–10% in recent years, well under the policy ceiling.

The longer view

Step back to a ten-year window and the total amount owed shows the same pattern at a bigger scale: a long flat stretch, then a step up when a major school project gets financed, then a slow decline as the bonds get paid down.

Total amount the town owes on long-term debt at the end of each fiscal year. The big step up from FY20 to FY21 is when the bonds for the new Elbridge Gerry School were issued. The chart stops at FY24 because that is the most recent audited annual report; the FY25 audit is expected in early 2027. Source: FY24 ACFR, page 121.

The breakdown of the $116.5 million owed at the end of FY24:

  • $108.8 million in bonds the town sold to fund the projects voters approved (this includes a $7.7 million accounting adjustment for premiums collected when the bonds were sold above face value).
  • $0.5 million in a small capital financing taken out in FY24.
  • $3.5 million the Water Department owes to the state water authority (MWRA) for past projects. Paid out of water rates, not property taxes. No interest.
  • $3.7 million the Municipal Light Department owes through a pooled electric-utility loan (MMWEC). Paid out of electric rates, not property taxes.

The town also had $2.3 million in short-term notes outstanding at year-end. Those bridge the gap while bigger long-term bonds are being lined up.

About that FY30 row. The annual report shows FY30 through FY34 lumped together because the lump is how bond schedules are conventionally published. It is not a sudden jump in what the town pays. The five-year block totals $41.5 million, which works out to $8.3 million per year, the same range as the FY25 through FY29 years that come right before it. After that, payments step down: roughly $5.5 million per year FY35–39, then $1.9 million per year FY40–44, and by FY44 the current bonds are fully paid off (assuming voters do not approve more borrowing between now and then).

So the picture is the opposite of a jump. The annual payment is highest in FY25 ($11.0 million), holds in the $9 million range through FY29, drops to about $8.3 million for the FY30–34 stretch, and then keeps stepping down.

Voted but not yet borrowed

Voters have already approved another $12.23 million of borrowing that the town has not yet issued bonds for. When those bonds get sold, the resulting payments get added to the schedule above. The biggest pieces:

  • Roads and sidewalks: $8.98 million
  • Gerry School (remaining balance from the original authorization): $1.62 million
  • Water Department projects: $1.02 million
  • Mary Alley building roof: $480,000
  • Franklin Street Fire Station roof: $131,000

How the debt has performed

The size of the town's debt has grown over the last decade, but so have the things that pay for it. Two ratios that the state and bond rating agencies watch:

"Home values" is the total assessed value of all property in town, divided into general-obligation bonds outstanding (slightly less than total debt because it excludes the Water and Municipal Light direct borrowings, which are paid from those funds, not from property taxes). The pattern: from 2015 to 2024 the dollar amount Marblehead owed went up by 57%, from $74 million to $116 million. But total personal income in town grew by 66% and total assessed property value grew by 72% over the same period. So even though the debt got bigger in absolute terms, it got smaller as a share of the underlying economy.

Standard & Poor's, the agency that rates municipal bonds, has held Marblehead at AAA (the highest rating) with a stable outlook throughout this period. S&P revisited the rating in April 2026 and affirmed AAA but moved the outlook to negative, citing the FY27 operating-budget gap.

State law caps how much general obligation debt a town can carry at 5% of its equalized property valuation. For Marblehead at the end of FY24 that ceiling was about $475 million. The town was using about 12.5% of its legal capacity. Ten years earlier the town was at 30.9% of capacity; the equalized valuation grew faster than the debt did, so headroom kept widening.

Interest rates

The town's outstanding general obligation bonds carry interest rates in the 2.0% to 5.0% range. Most of them were issued between 2015 and 2021, a period of historically low interest rates, so the locked-in cost of carrying that debt is low by current standards. (For context, the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield, a common benchmark, ranged from about 1.5% in 2020 to about 4.3% in early 2024.) The town has also done several rounds of refunding over the years, the municipal equivalent of refinancing a mortgage, to swap older higher-cost bonds for newer lower-cost ones.

What this means for the operating override

The override question on the June 2026 ballot is about the day-to-day operating budget. It is a different decision from debt for two concrete reasons:

  1. The annual debt bill is already paid out of a separate, voter-approved tax. In FY23, $11.00 million of $11.00 million of debt service came from the exclusion side. Voting yes or no on the operating override does not change the debt payment. And cutting the operating budget does not free up money to spend on debt, because the operating budget was not paying for debt in the first place.
  2. The schedule for paying off existing bonds is already set. The 20-year table above is contractually owed. The town's main flexibility on debt is either refunding (refinancing at lower rates, which it has done) or asking voters to approve new borrowing on future projects.

The maturity schedule above is the legal payoff calendar for what voters have already approved. What it looks like in 2030, 2035, and 2040 depends on whether voters approve more borrowing between now and then, and on what scale. The 50-of-51 record is one signal about how Marblehead has voted on those questions historically. The next question will be its own ballot.
