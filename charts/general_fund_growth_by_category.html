Where the operating budget actually grew, FY18 to FY24

Five operating categories of spending, ranked by dollars added.

Marblehead's operating general fund (everything except voter-approved debt service) grew by about $16.6 million between FY18 and FY24. Most of that growth went to schools. Public safety wages added the second-largest chunk. The remaining three categories, "everything else," the budgetary pension line, and health insurance, each added smaller amounts on the order of $1 to $2 million.

Voter-approved debt service is intentionally excluded. It grew by $3.85 million over the same window, but each piece of that growth came from a separate ballot question (Bell School, Mary A. Alley, MHS roof, etc.) that authorized both the borrowing and the additional tax to pay for it. Debt service has its own funding stream outside the operating levy and is not in competition with operating departments for the same dollar.

Nominal dollar growth FY18 to FY24, decomposed from the polygon-encoded categories on where-has-the-money-gone.html (originally extracted from ACFR general fund schedules). Debt service growth ($3.85M, voter-approved capital) is excluded; the visible bars sum to about $16.6M of operating-side growth. Total general fund growth including debt was about $20.5M.

How to read this against the property tax levy. The town's property tax levy grew by about $11.8M over FY18 to FY24 (from $59.59M to $71.42M). The five operating bars above sum to $16.6M, more than the levy itself added. The gap was filled by non-levy revenue: state aid (Chapter 70 increases for schools), free cash drawn from reserves, local receipts, and one-time ARPA funds. So the operating budget did not just absorb the levy growth, it stretched beyond it using bandaids that have started to run out (free cash use was $10.2M in FY23 but is proposed at $5M for FY27).

Where the squeeze actually shows up. Schools and Public Safety wages together took $11.9M of the $16.6M operating growth, about 71%. The remaining three categories ("Everything else," budgetary pensions, and health insurance) together took just $4.8M. "Everything else" includes DPW, the library, the COA, general government (administration, town clerk, accounting, IT), public works support, and state and county charges. Several of those departments saw nominal-flat or near-flat budgets across the window, which is where the documented attrition lives (Engineering Department eliminated, DPW reduced by 12 positions, library hours cut, etc.).

Why "Pensions (budgetary)" looks small. The $1.65M shown is only the budgetary appropriation visible in the general fund expenditure schedule, not the full Essex Regional Retirement System assessment. The PERAC-tracked pension assessment grew from $11.83M (FY18) to $12.49M (FY24), about +$0.66M over the window, also small in absolute terms. Either accounting basis tells the same story for this window: pensions did not eat the operating budget.

Why debt service is not on the chart. Debt payments grew by $3.85M over this same window, the third-largest dollar growth after schools and public safety. That growth is funded by voter-approved debt exclusions (Bell School, Mary A. Alley, MHS roof and HVAC, Abbot Library renovation, fire pumper, Fort Sewall). Each exclusion is a separate ballot question that authorizes both the borrowing and the additional tax to pay for it. Debt service has its own funding stream outside the operating levy and was never available to fund operations, so including it in a chart about operating-side competition would be misleading. The dollars are real; the competition is not.

Caveat on the 2024 endpoint. FY2024 is the most recent year with closed-book ACFR data. The story since then is different and more acute: from FY26 to FY27, group insurance jumped 11% in a single year (a healthcare cliff event documented separately on healthcare costs). The chart above describes the trend that produced the documented attrition through FY24, not the immediate FY27 budget pressure.
