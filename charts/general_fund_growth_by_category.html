Where the operating budget actually grew, FY18 to FY24

Five operating categories of spending compared to the property tax levy.

The popular framing is that healthcare and pensions are eating Marblehead's budget. The data does not show that for the FY18 to FY24 window. The budgetary pension line and public safety wages outpaced the levy. Schools roughly kept pace with it. Health insurance and the catch-all "everything else" bucket (DPW, library, COA, general government, public works support, state and county charges) grew slower than the levy could.

Voter-approved debt service is intentionally excluded from this chart. It grew the most in dollar terms (+53.8%), but each piece of that growth came from a separate ballot question (Bell School, Mary A. Alley, MHS roof, etc.) that authorized both the borrowing and the additional tax to pay for it. Debt service has its own funding stream outside the operating levy and is not in competition with operating departments for the same dollar.

Nominal dollar growth FY18 to FY24, decomposed from the polygon-encoded categories on where-has-the-money-gone.html (originally extracted from ACFR general fund schedules). Levy growth from the town's levy series: $59.59M (FY18) to $71.42M (FY24), about 3.07% per year. Some of that levy growth is itself voter-approved debt exclusion additions, so the share of growth available to operating departments was smaller than the headline +19.9% suggests.

How to read this. The reference line at 19.9% is the property tax levy growth over FY18 to FY24. Categories above the line outgrew the levy in nominal dollars and consumed more than their proportional share of new revenue. Categories below the line lagged the levy and gave up share. The levy is the relevant benchmark for a Massachusetts municipality because Proposition 2½ caps how fast the levy can grow regardless of what costs do.

Why debt service is not on the chart. Debt payments grew +53.8% over the same window, the fastest of any category in the general fund. That growth is funded by voter-approved debt exclusions (Bell School, Mary A. Alley, MHS roof and HVAC, Abbot Library renovation, fire pumper, Fort Sewall). Each exclusion is a separate ballot question that authorizes both the borrowing and the additional tax to pay for it. Debt service has its own funding stream outside the operating levy and was never available to fund operations, so including it in a chart about operating-side competition would be misleading. The dollars are real; the competition is not.

Why "Pensions (budgetary)" looks small. The chart line marked "Pensions" reflects only the budgetary appropriation visible in the general fund expenditure schedule, not the full Essex Regional Retirement System assessment. The PERAC-tracked pension assessment grew from $11.83M (FY18) to $12.49M (FY24), about +5.6% over the window, well below the levy. The two figures differ because pension contributions are partly carried in funds outside the general fund operating view. Either accounting basis tells the same story for this window: pensions did not eat the operating budget.

What "Everything else" means. The remainder bucket after Schools, Public Safety, Pensions, and Health insurance. It includes DPW, the library, the COA, general government (administration, town clerk, accounting, IT), public works support, and state and county charges. This bucket grew about 6 percentage points slower than the levy. That is where the documented attrition (Engineering Department eliminated, DPW reduced by 12 positions, library hours cut, etc.) actually lives.

Caveat on the 2024 endpoint. FY2024 is the most recent year with closed-book ACFR data. The story since then is different and more acute: from FY26 to FY27, group insurance jumped 11% in a single year (a healthcare cliff event documented separately on healthcare costs). The chart above describes the trend that produced the documented attrition through FY24, not the immediate FY27 budget pressure.
