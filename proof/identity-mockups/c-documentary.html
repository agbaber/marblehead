Where the money comes from

Most of what the town spends is paid by you, on the house you live in. The rest fills small gaps.

Property tax dominates because Massachusetts law gives towns almost no other lever. Income and sales taxes belong to the state.

Marblehead collects about $123 million a year to run itself. Roughly four-fifths is the property tax bill you pay twice a year. The rest is a mix of state aid, local fees, and smaller sources that shift with the economy.

This concentration is a choice the state made for towns. Cities elsewhere lean on income or sales taxes; here, those belong to the state. What's left for the town is property — predictable, hard to dodge, and capped by law.

Why the cap matters

Proposition 2½ — a 1980 state law — limits how much the property-tax pot can grow each year. The pot can rise 2.5%, plus new construction added to the tax rolls. Anything beyond that requires an override: a separate ballot vote.

That gap is the reason the budget conversation feels constrained. Costs that aren't capped — health insurance, special education, pensions — grow at 5–9% a year. Revenue that is capped grows at 2.5%. The gap compounds.
