Lecture 03 / 08 Revenue

Where the money comes from

Most of what the town spends is paid by you, on the house you live in. The rest fills small gaps.

4 min read Updated June 2026

Marblehead collects about $123 million a year to run itself. Roughly four-fifths of that is the property tax bill you pay twice a year. The remaining fifth is a mix of state aid, local fees, and a handful of smaller sources that change with the economy.

This concentration is a choice the state of Massachusetts made for towns. Cities elsewhere lean on income or sales taxes; here, those belong to the state. What's left for the town is property — predictable, hard to dodge, and capped by law.

Why the cap matters

Proposition 2½ — a 1980 state law — limits how much the property-tax pot can grow each year. The pot can rise 2.5%, plus the value of any new construction added to the tax rolls. Anything beyond that requires an override: a separate ballot vote.

That cap is the reason the town's budget conversation feels constrained. Costs that aren't capped — health insurance, special education, pensions — grow at 5–9% a year. Revenue that is capped grows at 2.5%. The gap compounds.
