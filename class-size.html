How big is a first grade class?

Glover Elementary parents say the district plans to run first grade with three classrooms next year instead of four, so a teacher can move to cover a late-summer rise in kindergarten enrollment. That raises first grade class sizes from about 16 students to about 21. The math is simple: 64 children split three ways instead of four. The decision itself is not confirmed. No Marblehead School Committee agenda, minutes, or district statement mentions it as of July 15, 2026, so for now it is only what parents report.

What we could and couldn't confirm

Confirmed from primary records: Glover's incoming first-grade cohort is this year's kindergarten class of 64 students (state enrollment data). Glover ran four first-grade sections in 2025–26 (district staffing roster). Marblehead's teacher contract sets only a district-wide average of "25 pupils, more or less," with no grade-specific limit.

Parent-reported, not yet in any district document: the decision to move first grade from four sections to three; the driver being a fourth kindergarten section; and the figure that 16 of the 64 incoming first graders are high-needs learners. No School Committee posting through July 15, 2026 records the change, and it may be a school- or central-office staffing decision that would not necessarily appear in one.

  • The math is just 64 divided by 3 or 4. Glover's incoming first grade is 64 children. Four classrooms is 16 each; three classrooms is about 21 each.
  • Massachusetts sets no class-size limit for first grade. State regulation caps only kindergarten, at an average of 25. Grades 1 and up are left to local school committees and teacher contracts, which commonly land around 25.
  • 21 is right at the state median; 16 was well below it. The most recent state breakdown by grade puts the typical first-grade general-education class at 20 students.
  • 16-to-21 is close to the contrast the landmark study measured. Project STAR compared small classes of 13–17 against regular classes of 22–25. A class of 16 is a small class; 21 is one short of a regular one. STAR found the small classes worth a modest early-grade edge (about a quarter of a standard deviation), though studies outside STAR find smaller or mixed effects.

The Glover numbers

The count that drives everything is the size of the incoming first-grade class. That is this year's kindergarten cohort moving up. State data puts it at 64. Split that class into four rooms and each teacher has 16 children; split it into three and each has about 21.

The parents' letter also states that 16 of the 64 incoming first graders (25%) are high-needs learners, including 13 students with disabilities, and that kindergarten classes have both a teacher and an instructional assistant while first grade has a teacher only. Those specifics are not independently verifiable from public records, though the district's own November 2025 budget discussion did single out Glover for the intensity of its special-education services.

What Massachusetts requires

Massachusetts is one of the states that does not cap class size by law. State regulation sets a single number: kindergarten classes cannot exceed an average of 25. For first grade and above, there is no state maximum and no required teacher-to-student ratio.

Where a limit exists, it is almost always in a local teacher contract. Marblehead's is typical: the teachers' agreement (Unit A) commits the district to "an average class size of 25 pupils, more or less," and nothing more specific. It sets no separate number for first grade, and no separate number for a class with more high-needs students. The most recent contract, running through 2028, left that language unchanged. A class of 21 is inside that average; a class of 16 was comfortably below it.

Some districts add their own School Committee guideline on top of the contract. Marblehead does not appear to have one setting grade-specific class-size targets, so the "25, more or less" average is the only written standard the decision has to satisfy.

Where 21 sits against the rest of the state

The state does not publish a current average class size broken out by grade. Its most recent statewide figure, 17.2 students for 2024–25, folds in special-education, English-learner, and small elective sections, so it runs below the size of a regular first-grade homeroom and is not the right yardstick here.

The last time the state broke class size out by grade for general-education classes, in 2016–17, the typical first-grade class was 20 students, rising slightly through the elementary grades. By that measure a 21-student first grade is a hair above the state median, and the 16-student version was well below it.

Affluent peer districts run their early grades on informal targets in the same range. Needham treats a K–3 class of 19 or fewer as having room to take a transfer student, an operating ceiling near 20. The Martha's Vineyard teachers' contract sets a maximum of 25 but requires a staff review whenever a K–3 class passes 20. Both suggest that a first grade in the low 20s is common but sits at the upper edge of what these districts aim for in the earliest grades.

What the research does and doesn't say

The evidence on class size is real but narrower than either side of an argument tends to claim. The strongest study is Tennessee's Project STAR, a 1980s experiment that randomly assigned kindergarten-through-third-grade students and their teachers to small classes of 13–17 or regular classes of 22–25. Students in the small classes gained roughly two to three months of extra learning, about one-fourth of a standard deviation, and the benefit was largest in the earliest grades. That finding is the basis for the widely shared claim that small early-grade classes matter.

That finding is fairly close to the Glover case. A class of 16 falls inside STAR's small-class range, and 21 sits just below its regular-class range, so moving first grade from 16 to 21 is close to the small-versus-regular contrast STAR measured, roughly a quarter of a standard deviation in the early grades. Two things keep it from being a clean match. First, 21 is one student below STAR's 22–25 regular class, so the expected effect is a little smaller than STAR's full gap. Second, the size of the effect is contested: a systematic review outside STAR found only a small effect on reading and no clear effect on math, and skeptics argue the gains are modest except in the very smallest classes. A class of 16 was already inside the range STAR found beneficial; 21 is not.

There is also a trade-off that cuts the other way. Adding a classroom means hiring another teacher, and studies of large class-size-reduction programs, including California's in the 1990s, found that the scramble to fill new positions can lower average teacher quality enough to offset part of the benefit of the smaller class. The honest summary: smaller classes in the early grades probably help a little, the help is clearest for classes well under 20, and the research neither shows that 16 was necessary nor that 21 is harmful.

Three ways to read this

The numbers above are not in dispute. What people disagree about is what weight to give them.

The parents' case

First grade is where children learn to read independently and build number sense, and it is the point where a class of high-needs learners most needs small groups and individual attention. Going from 16 to 21 removes a teacher from a cohort the parents describe as one-quarter high-needs, at the same time the school is without a permanent principal and has no assistant principal. Whatever the statewide median, the change makes this particular class larger and thinner-staffed at the worst moment to do it.

The operational case

A district has a fixed number of teachers and has to place them where the children are. If kindergarten enrollment rises late in the summer, a fourth kindergarten room has to come from somewhere, and first grade at 21 stays at the state median and well inside the contract's average of 25. On this reading the change is a reallocation forced by enrollment, not a cut, and 21 is an ordinary first-grade class almost anywhere in Massachusetts.

What the evidence supports

The strongest study measured almost this contrast: 16 is a small class and 21 is close to a regular one, and the small classes showed a modest early-grade edge. So a class of 16 likely carried a real early-literacy advantage over a class of 21. But that edge is an average across thousands of students, not a guarantee for any one classroom; it is small and its size is contested; and adding a teacher has its own costs. The research does not show that 21 harms first graders, nor that 16 was required. It leaves the decision a judgment call, not a question the data answers on its own.

Open questions

  1. Is there a district record of the decision? As of July 15, 2026 no School Committee agenda or minutes, and no district statement, records the four-to-three change. It may be a school-level staffing decision that never reaches the committee. The next posted agenda or a direct answer from the superintendent's office would confirm it.
  2. Surge or decline? The reported driver is a late-summer rise in kindergarten enrollment, but the district's own FY27 budget narrative repeatedly cites declining enrollment to explain why cut positions were not restored. Reconciling those two pictures would clarify whether this is a genuine one-year bulge or a placement choice.
  3. How many high-needs students, and how are they distributed? The 16-of-64 figure comes from the parents' letter. The district has not published a high-needs count for the cohort, and how those students are spread across three or four rooms matters as much as the raw class size.
  4. What did the spring staffing plan assume? If the budget passed last spring funded four first-grade sections at Glover, the district planned for 16, not 21. Whether that was a firm commitment or a placeholder against uncertain enrollment is the difference between a reversal and a routine summer adjustment.
