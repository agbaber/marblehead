Is the light department well run?

Marblehead has owned its own electric utility since 1894. The Marblehead Municipal Light Department (MMLD) publishes an annual report to the Town every year; the fifteen reports covering 2010 through 2024 are the department's own public record of what it spent, what broke, and what it told residents. That record shows a utility with strong finances, third-party reliability awards, and rising maintenance spending. It also documents the things residents are sore about: a 34% rate increase over nine months in 2022, a regulatory shutdown of the town's emergency generators that the reports never mentioned while it was happening, and a building renovation that needed half a million dollars of follow-up fixes.

Nothing in fifteen years of the department's own reports documents financial trouble: no operating losses, growing reserves, debt only for one building project. The documented grievances are about disclosure and rates, not money going missing. Both halves of that sentence matter.

The reports changed in 2018

The annual reports come in two eras. Through 2017 they run one to two thousand words of narrative, mostly weather recaps and renewable-energy news, with no outage counts, no reliability numbers, and no project budgets. From 2018 on, under a new general manager, they roughly double in length and start disclosing outage events with customer counts and restoration times, competitive bid results with vendor names and dollar amounts, equipment delivery delays, and month-by-month board decisions.

Joseph Kowalik became general manager in 2018, succeeding Andrew Hadden (2014–2018), who had succeeded Jay Anderson and, before him, Robert Jolly. The disclosure gap between the two eras matters for everything below: the thin years are thin because the reports said little, not necessarily because little happened.

Outages and storm response

The post-2018 reports disclose every major outage event. The record, in the department's own numbers:

  • March 2–3, 2018 nor'easter (a federally declared disaster): 11 outage events, 265 customers, average restoration 13.1 hours, which the report calls "much longer than MMLD's typical response time." The storm also eroded the Lead Mills berm carrying the supply lines from Salem; MMLD paid $135,240 to repair it and filed over $200,000 in federal disaster-reimbursement claims.
  • August 24, 2018, the self-inflicted one: three rapid outages at the Commercial Street substation knocked out 1,462 customers, about 14% of the town, for 90 to 150 minutes. An outside vendor's diagnosis: dust from MMLD's own building renovation, plus rainwater moisture, caused an electro-mechanical component to fail.
  • July 17, 2019 microburst: 20 outages, 1,670 customers (16% of town), last customers restored 24 to 30 hours in, with mutual-aid line crews from four other towns.
  • October 17, 2019 "bomb cyclone": 64 outages, 2,124 customers (20% of town), the last 18 customers out for 48 to 72 hours.
  • October 27, 2021 nor'easter: trees fell on the main supply lines from Salem at 2:30 a.m. and the department deliberately de-energized the whole town to clear them safely. Everyone had power back by 4 p.m. Direct cost, including overtime, mutual aid, tree crews, and lost sales: about $90,000.

Against that storm record sit third-party reliability numbers. The American Public Power Association (APPA), the national association of municipal utilities, gave MMLD a Certificate of Excellence in Reliability for 2021, 2022, and 2023. The metric is SAIDI, the average minutes of outage per customer per year. MMLD's 2022 figure was under 38 minutes, 19% better than the average of 66 New England municipal utilities surveyed; its 2023 figure was under 17 minutes, 69% better than Northeast peers and 76% better than similar-sized municipal utilities nationwide.

The structural weak point the reports keep returning to is not the poles in town. It is the supply path: every grid-delivered electron enters Marblehead through the Village 13 substation, fed by overhead lines along the old Salem railroad right-of-way. That is what failed in October 2021 and what eroded at Lead Mills in 2018. The department's current capital program targets exactly this: a Village 13 rebuild with 50% more transformer capacity (roughly $9.7 million committed across switchgear, transformers, and site work in 2022–2024) and a $1.98 million federal grant application to put steel poles on the Salem end and bury the Marblehead end of the supply lines.

Maintenance spending: flat, then a sustained ramp

The clearest maintenance series in the financial statements is the line-maintenance account, which covers upkeep of the overhead and underground wires themselves. It was roughly flat around $700,000 to $780,000 a year through 2017, then climbed every year after: up about 120% from 2016 to 2024.

Maintenance of Overhead & Underground Lines, from the Operating Expenses statement in each year's annual report. The posted PDFs for 2011 and 2013–2015 contain the narrative letter only, no financial statements; those years are shown as dashed gaps, not zeros.

What sits behind the ramp, per the reports themselves: the 2018–2019 storm damage, a general step-up in preventive replacement (68 poles set in 2019 against 23 in 2018, a jump the 2019 report itself points out), and the department's first dedicated tree-trimming contract, signed in June 2022 with Mayer Tree Service at up to $300,000 a year. A resident can read the timing two ways: spending rose when it needed to, or a town of overhead lines and mature trees did not have a systematic tree-trimming program until 2022, the year after falling trees blacked out the whole town. Both readings are consistent with the record.

The Wilkins Plant: a 3.5-year shutdown the reports didn't mention

MMLD owns two 2.5-megawatt diesel generators at the transfer station, the Wilkins Plant. They earn money supporting the regional grid on peak days, and they are the only generation in town capable of powering residents in an emergency.

The Massachusetts Department of Environmental Protection (DEP) prohibited the plant from operating starting in November 2015, pending compliance with air-emissions and noise regulations. That prohibition is disclosed for the first time in the 2018 annual report, the first one signed by the new general manager. DEP cleared the plant to resume normal operation in March 2019.

The 2015, 2016, and 2017 reports, covering the years the prohibition was actually in force, say only this: "we are currently performing upgrades to the system, allowing these units to run as clean, quiet, and efficiently as possible," while continuing to describe the diesels as "a proven resource for the town... supplying the town with emergency power if necessary." A regulator had ordered the town's only emergency generation offline for three and a half years, and the annual reports to the Town described it as routine upgrades until after a management change.

For anyone asking "do they tell us when something is wrong," this is the concrete before-and-after case. The same reports that hid the Wilkins prohibition era also carried no outage data; the reports since 2018 disclose a self-caused substation failure, vendor delivery delays, and renovation defects on their own.

The $8.7 million building renovation, and its rework

MMLD renovated its 1894 headquarters at 80 Commercial Street between 2016 and 2018. The 2013 through 2016 reports describe the design process, the architect, and the bidding. None of them state a project budget. The total appears for the first time in the 2018 report, after completion: $8.7 million, paid with $4.2 million from reserves and a $4.5 million, 15-year loan at 3.31% interest. The loan was also the department's first debt in decades; the 2010 through 2014 reports had each noted that plant investment had been achieved "without the need to issue debt."

What the later reports disclose about the renovation's quality:

  • August 2018: renovation dust contributed to the substation failure that blacked out 1,462 customers (above).
  • 2023: $235,000 approved for exterior capital improvements "not included in the 2016-18 renovation."
  • 2024: $283,829 contract to fix "roof and downspout drainage issues not adequately addressed in 2016-18 renovation contract."

That is roughly $519,000 of follow-up work on an $8.7 million project, in the department's own words, plus one sizable outage. Not scandal-scale, and disclosed voluntarily, but it is the documented file for "the building project had real problems."

Rates: a decade of stability, then the 2022 spike

One honest way to see the customer's cost across fifteen years is operating revenue divided by electricity sold. This is an average across all customer classes, not any single bill, but it moves when rates move.

Derived figure; it averages all rate classes and includes non-energy revenue. 2011–2015 financial statements are not in the posted PDFs (dashed gap). The 2016 point uses the 2016 report's own consumption figure; the 2017 report states a different, lower figure for 2016, one of several small inconsistencies in the pre-2018 reports.

The 2022 jump is the story. Wholesale power costs are passed to customers through a line on the bill called the purchased power adjustment (the "PPA" line). In 2022, as natural gas prices spiked after Russia invaded Ukraine, MMLD raised that adjustment three times: 3.1 to 4.1 cents per kWh in March, to 5.6 cents in August, to 9.0 cents in October. The department's own report calls the result "a 34% residential rate increase from January to October." By June 2023 the adjustment had fallen back to 0.5 cents.

The driver was the regional wholesale market, not MMLD operations: purchased power cost the department $10.5 million in 2021 and $12.7 million in 2022 while sales fell slightly. But the pass-through mechanism gave customers no cushioning, and the fund built for exactly this purpose, the rate stabilization reserve, sat at about $900,000, roughly one month of wholesale costs, through the entire spike. The board tripled it to $3.3 million in 2024, on the advice of the department's auditors and APPA, after the shock had passed.

Two other rate changes land on top of that. In January 2023 and again in January 2024, following a consultant's cost-of-service study, the monthly fixed charge on a residential bill rose from $4.25 to $11.25 and then to $18.50, with the per-kWh rate trimmed to hold total revenue in each class constant. Revenue-neutral in total, but not per household: a fixed charge that quadruples in two years falls hardest on low-usage customers, and it lengthens the payback on rooftop solar. The study's rationale, that fixed costs should be covered by fixed charges, is standard utility economics. The resentment it generates is also standard.

What the town gets: $330,000, every year, for fifteen years

MMLD makes an annual payment in lieu of taxes (PILOT) to the Town. In every one of the fifteen reports, 2010 through 2024, that payment is exactly $330,000. It has never been raised. Over the same period the department's retained surplus grew from $21.1 million to $23.6 million, and its cash plus depreciation fund grew from about $6 million to over $15 million.

A resident can fairly ask why the town's dividend has been flat for fifteen years while reserves grew. The department's implicit answer is in the capital program: those reserves paid cash for roughly $9.7 million of the Village 13 substation rebuild without new borrowing. Neither the question nor the answer appears in any of the reports; the number just never changes. (For balance-sheet readers: the surplus figure drops sharply between the 2018 and 2019 reports, from $31.8 million to $14.9 million. That is an accounting restatement that put pension and retiree-health liabilities on the balance sheet for the first time, not money lost.)

The pattern: where a green investment had a clear payback (LEDs, smart meters, cheap hydro), the department took it and published the numbers. Where it didn't (local solar at twice the portfolio cost), the department declined and said why. The gaps are on the smaller programs, where costs (EV chargers) or benefits (rebates) go unquantified, and on Berkshire Wind, whose contract cost has never appeared in an annual report.

What residents actually think, measured

In late 2025 MMLD commissioned an independent survey of 350 residential customers (margin of error ±5.1%) as part of a statewide study that also surveyed customers of other municipal utilities and of investor-owned utilities like National Grid and Eversource. The results are not close: 89.7% overall satisfaction, 68.3% saying they trust the utility "completely," and a net promoter score of +67.4, far above both the Massachusetts municipal-utility average and the investor-owned utilities on nearly every measure.

The texture under the headline matches the folk impression that residents love the trucks and grumble about everything else:

  • The highest-rated attributes are exactly the storm ones: 96.2% positive on "providing consistent and reliable electric service" and 95.7% on "restoring power after an outage in a reasonable amount of time."
  • The lowest-rated attribute is "offering innovative programs and services" at 61.8%, the only category where MMLD scores below the Massachusetts municipal-utility average.
  • On rates, 69.4% call them reasonable, but the largest single block (39.4%) picks "somewhat reasonable," the lukewarm option.
  • On the green transition, 54.9% say MMLD is doing enough to curb carbon emissions, and a large 38.9% say they don't know. Only 35.1% had heard of the rebate programs.

Fast restoration is visible from a kitchen window. Portfolio strategy, rate design, and reserve policy are visible only in documents most residents never read. The satisfaction gap between those two categories is the trust gap.

What this record can't show

Everything above comes from documents the department wrote about itself, plus one commissioned survey. Real limits:

  • Missing financials. The PDFs posted on the department's website for 2011 and 2013–2015 contain the narrative letter only. The financial statements for those years exist in the printed Town Reports and could complete the series.
  • No independent operations data here. The federal Energy Information Administration collects reliability (SAIDI) and rate data from every utility, and every Massachusetts municipal light plant files an annual return with the state Department of Public Utilities. Both would let the reliability awards and rate history be checked against non-MMLD sources.
  • No cost detail on the power contracts. The annual reports name every supply contract but price almost none of them. Board meeting minutes, posted on the department's website back to 2015, carry more of that detail.
  • Small-number sloppiness in the older reports. The 2016 report states 2016 consumption as 104.77 million kWh; the 2017 report states 102.85 million for the same year. The 2016 and 2017 reports also miscount their own cumulative PILOT totals ("thirteen-year" for fourteen and fifteen years). Minor, but worth knowing when quoting pre-2018 figures.

The source PDFs and extracted text for all fifteen reports and the survey are archived in this project's light department source notes, alongside the working analysis this page is built from.
